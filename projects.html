Optical-to-Electrical Receiver

May 2026 – Present • Hardware Designer • PCB Design • Analog Circuit Design • LTspice

Designed and experimentally validated a low-power optical-to-electrical receiver for converting photodiode current into 12 steps of a controlled voltage output. The component selection and circuit topology was optimized around competing requirements for bandwidth, linearity, and power consumption.

Project details

Design Specifications

The receiver was designed around a constrained set of technical specifications. Rather than optimizing a single circuit parameter, I balanced bandwidth, power, supply voltage, transistor operating region, and output resolution to produce a practical receiver architecture. I designed two boards: one to target visible light with a 50KHz bandwidth and one to target near-IR fiber-optic frequencies with a 100KHz bandwidth.

100 kHz Target bandwidth
5 mW Power consumption
5 V Supply voltage
0 – 3.5 μA Base current range
300 mV Voltage increment
12 Discrete voltage steps

3D renderings, routing diagram, and final product for a PCB board of a common emitter amplifier circuit.

Operating-Point Optimization

A central design challenge was selecting a transistor operating point that could accommodate the photodiode's current output while maintaining sufficient linearity and voltage swing.

I characterized the transistor response by sweeping the base current and measuring the resulting collector voltage. This allowed me to set collector and feedback ressitor values that would ensure the transistor's linear region matched the expected photodiode current range. I then adjusted the feedback path to refine the operating point without negatively impacting gain and power consumption.

The final design reduced power consumption from approximately 18 mW to 5 mW without sacrificing linearity or the ~4V voltage headroom. This required balancing the reduction in bias current against the resulting changes in gain, linearity, bandwidth, and usable dynamic range rather than simply minimizing current draw.

Simulation & Frequency-Response Analysis

I developed models for both PCBs in LTspice and used simulation to evaluate the circuit's operating point, gain, feedback behavior, and frequency response before and after fabrication.

The initial measured frequency response differed from the simulated high-frequency PCB model. I traced the gain through the circuit to identify the source of the mismatch, which revealed that the current mirror gain had been overlooked in my calculations of transimpedance gain. This made the transimpedance bandwidth look higher than it actually was.

Experimental characterization of the PNP current mirror using a voltage sweep on the reference transistor's collector.

Initial mismatch between simulated and observed bandwidth. Observed bandwidth is artifically increased because of current mirror gain, which was not factored into the calculations.

I independently characterized the current mirror on the bench and compared its measured behavior with simulation. Incorporating the frequency-dependent current-mirror response into the analysis allowed me to correct the observed bandwidth and have it match the simulating bandwidth of 50KHz. I altered the current mirror reference resistor increasing bandwidth from 30KHz to 200KHz. This change in the current mirror driver increased the bandwdith of my CEA by 2x.

Photodiode Interface & Linear Region

The receiver was designed to interface directly with the current output of a photodiode. I optimized the transistor's operating region so that its response remained useful across the expected input-current range, allowing the circuit to translate small changes in photodiode current into a predictable voltage response.

Visuo-Tactile Sensing System for Robotics

December 2026 – Present • Lead Researcher • Rapid Prototyping • Mechanical Design • Optical Systems

Established proof of concept for a novel visuo-tactile sensor using photoelastic polymers. Developed and characterized the system, iterating across mechanical design, optical architecture, and experimental instrumentation to maximize force sensitivity and spatial resolution.

Project details

Design Specifications

The visuo-tactile sensing system relies on the photoelastic response of ClearFlex 50 to visualize applied forces as changes in birefringence. When viewed between crossed polarizers, mechanical stress produces characteristic fringe patterns and color shifts that can be captured by a camera and analyzed to estimate both the magnitude and direction of applied forces. Since the fringe patterns are due to molecular-scale interactions, the sensing system offers the potential for superior spatial resolution compared to resistive and capacitive sensing. This makes is suitable for high-precision applications such as robotic medical procedures, slip detection, and material recognition. The following design specifications are suited for these applications.

1 mm Spatial resolution
200 mW Power consumption
0.5 N Minimum force sensitivity
5 cm x 5 cm Maximum footprint
1000+ Loading cycles
<20ms Feedback latency

Iterative Testing

Iterative Testing

The initial design prioritized proof-of-concept over miniaturization. A thin flexible PLA layer with 3D-printed spikes was positioned above the photoelastic gel, while a TFT display provided illumination from the rear. This established that localized deformation could generate a measurable optical response, but the size of the TFT screen limited the form factor.

Increasing sensitivity required concentrating optical and mechanical deformation within a small sensing region. However, concentrating the deformation too aggressively can reduce spatial resolution by causing neighboring sensing regions to interact. I therefore iterated on spike geometry, illumination, and gel configuration to balance force sensitivity against spatial resolution.

After establishing proof of concept, I moved on to testing the force sensitivity and resolution of the sensor through several 3D printed test setups.

Both setups involved placing known masses on top of the polymer and measuring the intensity of fringe patterns with an iPhone camera. A major issue was the large focusing distance of the camera (>12 cm) which led me to switch to a smaller Raspberry Pi camera module. The data was processed in ImageJ.

Photoelastic properties

Photoelastic Properties

The tactile sensing system uses ClearFlex 50, a photoelastic liquid urethane rubber. At rest, the chains are randomly oriented. When a load is applied, the internal polymer molecules stretch and align themselves along the paths of the internal mechanical stress. This creates two separate refractive indexes within the material, which splits incident light into two waves, which recombine at the analyzer and constructively or destructively interfere. Different frequencies of light have different areas of interference, which is why the fringes are colored.

The photoelastic response also provides information about the direction of loading. Tensile and compressive deformation produce distinguishable spectral shifts, allowing the system to differentiate between opposing force directions rather than simply measuring force magnitude.

Linear Actuator

For more rigorous testing of the polymer's force sensitivity, I designed and built a custom linear actuator. The machine is driven by a NEMA-17 stepper motor and the parts were designed by me in Fusion 360.

Polargraph Machine

October 2025 • Hardware Designer • Rapid Prototyping • Machine Building • Embedded Systems

Worked in a team of three to build a Polargraph machine capable of reproducing a user's drawing on an iPad. Iterated on the end-effector, developed an embedded motion control system, and designed a custom 3D-printed chassis enabling precise two-axis motion using stepper motors.

Chassis Design

Chassis Design

I designed a chassis with a timing belt clamping mechanism built in and secured by a set screw. The chassis slid back and forth on an aluminum extrusion on four wheels. I opted for laser cutting instead of 3D printing the chassis base to accelerate iteration while optimizing the spacing between the V-wheels. The final geometry was tuned to minimize friction against the aluminum extrusion while maintaining a secure constraint, with an eccentric nut providing fine adjustment of the wheel preload. I added two pockets for linear bearings with set screw retention to have the end effector slide out of the chassis on metal rods.

Close-up of the polargraph machine reproducing a circle.

Iterative Testing

Iterative Testing

During testing, our prototype repeatedly failed geometric torture tests because its heavy end-effector caused poor cornering accuracy. I redesigned the mechanism, reducing its mass by 100g by replacing an actively actuated pen-lift with a spring-assisted gravity drop and a smaller reset servo. I also implemented microstepping using A4988 motor drivers, which reduced low-speed motor resonance by ~70% without sacrificing speed. The re-designed passed every torture test and won the competition due to its high precision.
